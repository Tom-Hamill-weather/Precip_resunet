# Review of "Six-hourly Probabilistic Precipitation Forecasts Created from Hourly Probabilities" (method_6h.tex)

## Overall assessment

This is a clean, well-scoped methods note describing a lightweight MLP that aggregates six hourly Gamma-mixture forecasts into a six-hourly Gamma-mixture forecast, evaluated against a sensible independence-assumption control. The core idea is good, the control construction is a real strength (it isolates "did the MLP learn temporal dependence" from "is the underlying hourly model good"), and the reliability-diagram results are internally consistent with the figures. The manuscript is **not yet publishable as-is**: it contains a direct contradiction between the Results and Summary sections about what the performance diagrams show, an unresolved discrepancy in the stated input dimensionality that propagates through a figure, a dimensionally nonsensical description of the NLL cap, two citations with DOIs that resolve to the wrong papers, and no uncertainty quantification on the BSS differences that are the paper's central quantitative claim. None of these are fatal to the underlying method, but all should be fixed before submission.

Main strength: the independence-assumption control is a genuinely useful and clearly-motivated diagnostic, and the finding that the advantage is concentrated in calibration rather than discrimination (Sec. 4) is a nice, honest, non-oversold result.

Main weakness: the write-up was evidently assembled from different drafting passes that were not fully reconciled — the 36-vs-38 input-dimension inconsistency and the Results/Summary contradiction on performance diagrams both look like leftover text from an earlier version of the analysis.

## Major issues

1. **Results and Summary directly contradict each other on what the performance diagrams show.**
   Location: Results, lines 520–527 and 542–544, vs. Summary, lines 641–646.
   In Results, the text says: "the MLP and control curves lie close together at both lead times and for all three thresholds, frequently overlapping or crossing. Maximum CSI values differ by 0.01 or less... at some thresholds — most noticeably the 10 mm exceedance at 12-h lead time — the control's curve sits marginally up and to the right of the MLP's," and concludes "the MLP's demonstrated advantage over the naive independence assumption lies in probabilistic calibration rather than in event discrimination."
   The Summary instead asserts: "the MLP traces a curve with higher probability of detection at a given success ratio, and therefore higher critical success index, than the independence-assumption control at both lead times and all three thresholds, with the gap widening for the heavier, more temporally coherent precipitation events."
   These are opposite claims (curves are indistinguishable / control sometimes better, vs. MLP is uniformly and increasingly better). I checked the actual figures (`PerfDiagram_6h_MLP_MRMS_3panel_..._12h.png` and `..._48h.png`) and they support the Results-section description, not the Summary's. **Fix**: rewrite the Summary paragraph (lines 641–650) to match the Results section's own, correct conclusion — the performance-diagram advantage is negligible/mixed, and the real advantage is in calibration (BSS), not discrimination.

2. **Input feature dimension is inconsistently stated as 38 vs. 36, and Fig. 2 depicts only 36 inputs.**
   Locations: Sec. 1 (line 103), Sec. 2.1 (line 159, Eq. 2), Sec. 3.1 (line 251, Eq. 5, "38 → 72 → ...") all say the input is **38**-dimensional (36 hourly parameters + 2 cyclic day-of-year features). But Fig. 2's caption (lines 273–277, "maps 36 input features... through five hidden layers") and the diagram itself (`Figs_6h/mlp_architecture_6h.png`, which shows exactly 6 input nodes × 6 "h" = 36, with no day-of-year nodes at all), Sec. 3.4 (line 347, "Each of the 36 input features is independently standardized"), and the Summary (line 614, "compact MLP with architecture 36 → 72 → 144 → 72 → 36 → 12 → 6") all say **36** and omit the seasonal features entirely.
   I checked `train_6hourly_mlp.py` in the repo: `N_INPUT = 36 + len(SEASONAL_VARS) = 38`, confirming 38 is the actual/correct value. **Fix**: add two input nodes (cos/sin day-of-year) to the Fig. 2 diagram and correct its caption to 38; fix line 347 and line 614 to read 38 (and "38 → 72 → 144 → 72 → 36 → 12 → 6").

3. **"Per-sample NLL values are restricted to a maximum of 100 mm" is dimensionally wrong and will confuse readers.** (line 329–330)
   NLL is a log-likelihood, not a precipitation amount, so it cannot have units of mm. I checked the code (`train_6hourly_mlp.py` line 232: `nll = torch.clamp(nll, max=100.0)`) — the cap of 100 is applied to the (unitless) NLL value itself, not to a precipitation amount. **Fix**: change to "restricted to a maximum value of 100" (drop "mm"), or clarify explicitly that it is the loss value, not a precipitation quantity, that is capped.

4. **Unexplained, unjustified change in MRMS quality threshold between training and verification.**
   Sec. 2.2 (lines 192–198) requires mean MRMS quality **> 0.6** for training samples, with an explicit rationale (accumulation compounds retrieval uncertainty across six hours). Sec. 4 (line 419) then verifies using quality **> 0.5**. No reason is given for using a more lenient threshold at verification than at training, and it is not obvious a priori that this is innocuous (a laxer verification threshold could let in more low-quality radar pixels than were ever seen in training, potentially degrading reported reliability, or conversely diluting a stricter training-quality signal). **Fix**: either use the same threshold for both, or explicitly justify the difference and ideally show the results are insensitive to it.

5. **No uncertainty quantification on the BSS comparisons, which are the paper's central quantitative claim.**
   The reported MLP-vs-control BSS margins range from substantial (0.423 vs. 0.382 at 0.25 mm/48 h) down to very small (0.291 vs. 0.288 at 10 mm/12 h — a difference of 0.003; 0.177 vs. 0.173 at 10 mm/48 h — a difference of 0.004). The text nonetheless states "The MLP's BSS exceeds the control's at every threshold examined" (line 461) and the Summary leans on this to claim the MLP has learned "genuine temporal correlation structure" (line 648–650). Without a bootstrap or paired-difference confidence interval, a reviewer cannot tell whether the smallest margins (especially at the rare 10 mm threshold, where sample size for exceedances is necessarily small) are distinguishable from sampling noise. **Fix**: add bootstrap (e.g., resampling over independent synoptic cases/days) confidence intervals on the BSS differences, or a paired significance test.

6. **Two bibliography DOIs resolve to the wrong papers.**
   `hamill2023nbm` (line 740) is given doi:10.1175/MWR-D-22-0199.1, and `jesberg2023` (line 766) is given doi:10.1175/MWR-D-22-0200.1. I verified independently that the actual MWR 151(6) (2023) papers "Improving National Blend of Models Probabilistic Precipitation Forecasts... Part I: Methods" and "...Part II: Results" have manuscript IDs **MWR-D-22-0308.1** and **MWR-D-22-0310.1**, respectively (confirmed via AMS journal listing). The DOI actually printed for `jesberg2023` (10.1175/MWR-D-22-0200.1) resolves to an unrelated paper, "What Controlled the Low-Level Moisture Transport during the Extreme Precipitation in Henan Province of China in July 2021?" (same MWR issue). **Fix**: correct both DOIs to MWR-D-22-0308.1 and MWR-D-22-0310.1.

7. **Bibliography contains an uncited entry that is squarely on-topic.**
   `wilks1991` (Wilks, 1991, MWR, "Representing serial correlation of meteorological events and forecasts in dynamic decision-analytic models," lines 845–850) appears in `\begin{thebibliography}` but is never invoked with `\citet`/`\citep` anywhere in the running text (verified: every other bibliography key appears exactly twice — once as an in-text citation, once as the `\bibitem` — except this one, which appears only once). Given that it is directly relevant to the serial-correlation discussion in the Introduction (lines 78–91), right next to `wilks1990`, this looks like a citation the author intended to make and forgot, or a leftover from an earlier draft. **Fix**: either cite it (e.g., alongside `wilks1990` at line 88) or delete the entry.

8. **A directly relevant paper by the same author on multi-temporal-scale precipitation postprocessing is missing.** See Literature section below (Bellier et al. 2023). This is arguably the single most on-topic piece of prior work not cited, since it addresses exactly the "aggregate finer-resolution probabilistic guidance into coarser time windows" problem that motivates this manuscript, and Hamill is a coauthor.

## Minor issues / clarity

- **Lines 95–96**: "We thus seek an alternative, inspired by recent success with deep learning models for precipitation statistical postprocessing." This is asserted without a citation. Given that Sec. 1's citation list (lines 63–67) is all pre-2023 regression-style postprocessing, and the text is specifically invoking *deep learning* success, a citation here (e.g., Ghazvinian et al. 2022, already in the bibliography, or one of the newer DL papers identified in the Literature section below) would substantiate the claim rather than leaving it as a general appeal.
- **Lines 298–305** (component reordering / "hard structural reparameterization" of shape1/shape2 ordering): the explanation is workable but indirect ("the reordering decision is treated as a fixed operation during the backward pass... it does not itself contribute a gradient"). Using the standard term "stop-gradient" (or explicitly saying this is implemented as an index-based permutation with `.detach()`/no gradient through the sort) would be more precise and more immediately parseable to an ML-literate reader.
- **Eq. 6 / lines 224–232**: the importance-sampling proxy uses $1-\min_k p_{0,k}$ (the driest-hour probability) as a stand-in for "probability of positive precipitation somewhere in the window." This is a reasonable heuristic but is not the same as, e.g., $1-\prod_k p_{0,k}$ (probability of at least one wet hour under independence), which would arguably be a better proxy for wetness of the accumulation window. The choice of minimum over the six hours is not justified in the text; a sentence explaining why minimum was chosen over alternatives (simplicity? conservatism? sensitivity to the wettest single hour?) would help.
- **Lines 384–386**: "This single-Gamma initialization... is sufficient to achieve stable convergence given the smaller model size." This claim (that method-of-moments single-Gamma init is adequate here, unlike for the hourly ResUNet) is stated but not demonstrated. Was this compared against EM-based two-component initialization and found equivalent, or is it an untested assumption inherited by analogy to model size? A one-sentence clarification of the evidence behind this claim would help.
- **Sec. 3.6 (lines 393–397)**: the validation set is only 1 of 7 training days per month (day 5), i.e. roughly 12 site-days (24 initializations) of validation data across the full year. This is a defensible design (avoiding correlated random splits) but is a fairly small and possibly noisy basis for `ReduceLROnPlateau` and early stopping; a sentence acknowledging this trade-off (or reporting the stability of the resulting stopping epoch across lead times) would strengthen the training-details discussion.
- **Domain scope (Sec. 2.1, lines 134–137, vs. Sec. 2.3, line 207)**: the input data section states that GRAF covers "the contiguous United States (CONUS) and Europe," but the sampling section only gives a raw grid size, (1524, 1308), without stating which domain this is. (Cross-referencing the companion codebase, this is the CONUS-only grid.) A reader relying solely on this document would not know whether Europe data contributed to training/verification at all. **Fix**: state explicitly that training and verification use the CONUS domain only (or, if Europe is also used, say so).
- **Line 480–482**: "Other lead times and other thresholds were examined but are not presented here; forecasts were reliable and skillful for all forecasts examined, though reliability at higher thresholds sometimes deviated from the 1-1 line..." This sentence asserts a broader result (skill across all lead times) without any supporting figure, table, or number — see also "Unanswered questions" below. Even a compact summary figure (e.g., BSS vs. lead time, which appears to already exist in the repository as `plot_BSS_leadtime.py`/`BSS_leadtime_*.png`, based on the surrounding project) would substantiate this claim rather than leaving it as a bare assertion.
- **Fig. 6 caption / Fig. 7**: the six-hourly MLP CDF (median precipitation ≈ 25 mm) sits far to the right of all six individual hourly CDFs (each with essentially all its mass below ~10 mm) in `Figs_6h/6h_sample_IC2025120412_lead36h.png`. This is plausible physically (a persistently wet 6-hour window can sum to much more than any single hour's typical/modal value) but the magnitude of the shift is large enough that a skeptical reader may initially suspect an error. A sentence pointing out that this reflects a sustained, several-mm/hour rain event (i.e., explicitly connecting the panel to the implied per-hour intensity) would preempt that reaction.
- **Line 93–94**: "Papers such as these illustrate the problem of serial correlation but may not provide an actionable algorithm..." — "may not" reads as hedged/informal; consider "do not" if that is in fact the case, or specify which of the cited papers do vs. do not provide an implementable algorithm.

## Literature

- **DOI errors** for `hamill2023nbm` and `jesberg2023` — see Major issue 7 above. Correct DOIs are MWR-D-22-0308.1 and MWR-D-22-0310.1; the currently-printed jesberg2023 DOI (MWR-D-22-0200.1) belongs to an unrelated 2023 MWR paper on the Henan, China extreme-precipitation event.
- **Uncited bibliography entry** `wilks1991` — see Major issue 6.
- **`hughes1979` (Hughes and Sangster, 1979, MWR, "Combining precipitation probabilities")**: I was unable to independently confirm this citation via web search (pre-DOI-era MWR content from 1979 is poorly indexed online, and no search returned a direct hit). This does not mean it is fabricated — the level of specific detail given in the text (empirical joint-probability adjustments, warm-season vs. cold-season independence finding) reads like genuine familiarity with the paper — but I could not verify it independently, and I'd recommend the author double-check the exact volume/page/DOI against a AMS journals login or physical/library copy before submission.
- **Missing citation — directly on-topic, same author**: Bellier, J., B. Whitin, M. Scheuerer, J. Brown, and T. M. Hamill, 2023: "A Multi-Temporal-Scale Modulation Mechanism for the Postprocessing of Precipitation Ensemble Forecasts: Benefits for Streamflow Forecasting," *J. Hydrometeor.*, 24(4), doi:10.1175/JHM-D-22-0119.1. This paper is squarely about deriving multi-temporal-scale (i.e., multi-hour-window) precipitation postprocessing information from finer-resolution ensemble guidance — essentially the same problem this manuscript addresses (aggregate fine time-resolution probabilistic information into a coarser accumulation window while respecting temporal dependence) — and Hamill is a coauthor. It builds on the "canonical events" idea (J. Schaake, NWS/HEFS/MEFP), which is conceptually close to the "independence-assumption control" contrast drawn in this manuscript. This should be cited and, ideally, briefly contrasted with the MLP approach in the Introduction (near lines 78–100) and/or Summary (near lines 654–667, where streamflow/ensemble-scenario limitations are already discussed via Schaake et al. 2007).
- **Missing citation — closely related recent method**: Pic, R., C. Dombry, P. Naveau, and M. Taillardat, 2024: "Distributional Regression U-Nets for the Postprocessing of Precipitation Ensemble Forecasts," arXiv:2407.02125. This is a very recent (2024) precipitation-postprocessing paper doing distributional-parameter regression with a U-Net, methodologically adjacent to both this manuscript and its companion `hamill2026a`. Likely more relevant to cite in the companion hourly-ResUNet paper, but worth a mention here too given the shared "distributional-parameter output" theme.
- **Missing citation — recent DL precipitation-uncertainty paper**: Hu, W., M. Ghazvinian, W. E. Chapman, A. Sengupta, F. M. Ralph, and L. Delle Monache, 2023: "Deep Learning Forecast Uncertainty for Precipitation over the Western United States," *Mon. Wea. Rev.*, 151(6), 1367–1385, doi:10.1175/MWR-D-22-0268.1. Relevant as a recent deep-learning-based probabilistic QPF paper (and note Ghazvinian, already cited twice elsewhere in this manuscript for other work, is a coauthor), and would substantiate the "recent success with deep learning models for precipitation statistical postprocessing" claim at line 95–96.
- No other citation in the ~24-entry inline bibliography raised concerns; author names, journals, volumes, and page numbers I could verify via web search (Baran & Nemoda 2016; Ben Bouallègue 2013; Messner et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2017; Ghazvinian et al. 2022; Hamill et al. 2017; Hamill & Scheuerer 2018; Gascón et al. 2019; Ioffe & Szegedy 2015) matched the manuscript's bibliography entries.

## Unanswered questions

1. Why does the verification/reliability calculation (Sec. 4) use a MRMS quality threshold of >0.5, while training (Sec. 2.2) uses the stricter >0.6? Are results sensitive to this choice?
2. Are the reported BSS differences between the MLP and the independence control statistically distinguishable from sampling noise, particularly the small margins at the 10 mm threshold (0.003–0.004)? No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported anywhere.
3. The text says "other lead times and other thresholds were examined but are not presented" and asserts they were "reliable and skillful... for all forecasts examined" (lines 478–482) — what evidence supports this, and why not include a compact summary (e.g., a BSS-vs-lead-time panel) to substantiate the claim, especially since the paper explicitly restricts its shown results to just 12 h and 48 h?
4. Does the method generalize to other accumulation windows (3, 12, 24 h), as speculated in the Introduction (lines 98–100) and Summary (lines 654–658)? None of this is tested; is there a reason to expect the same architecture/training recipe to transfer, or would e.g. the hidden-layer sizing or importance-sampling weighting need to change for longer windows where zero-inflation is less dominant?
5. Does the approach generalize to the Europe domain, which the Input Data section (lines 134–137) states GRAF also covers? The sampling section's numbers (1524×1308 grid) appear to be CONUS-only (matching the companion codebase's CONUS grid dimensions); it is not stated whether Europe was excluded by design or simply not yet attempted.
6. How sensitive are the reported reliability/BSS results to the specific day-block train/test split (day 1–7 training, 8–9 gap, 10–end testing, replicated identically across all twelve months of a single year, 2025)? Only one year and one split scheme is evaluated; would a different holdout year or split produce materially different BSS numbers?
7. What is the actual parameter count and inference latency of the MLP? The abstract and title emphasize "lightweight," but no parameter count, FLOP count, or wall-clock inference time is given anywhere, despite the companion paper's evident interest in these metrics (per the architecture's own framing as an efficiency-motivated add-on to the hourly ResUNet).
8. How does the operational pipeline handle a missing or degraded one of the six hourly inputs (e.g., a GRAF/GFS data gap for one lead hour)? This is not discussed, and hourly data gaps are a known real-world occurrence for this modeling system.
9. What is the actual benefit, if any, of the two cyclic day-of-year features? No ablation (with vs. without) is reported, so the claim that they let the MLP "learn genuine seasonal variation" (lines 210–212) is asserted rather than demonstrated.
10. Similarly, no ablation is reported for the chosen hidden-layer widths, the bottleneck design, or the climatological output-layer initialization scheme — the Summary (lines 654–658) explicitly notes "The MLP architecture was not subject to extensive hyperparameter search," which is a fair and honest caveat, but a reviewer would still want to know how much these specific choices matter (e.g., does removing the climatological initialization change convergence speed or final skill measurably?).

## Figure-text consistency

- **Fig. 2 (`Figs_6h/mlp_architecture_6h.png`) and its caption**: shows/describes 36 inputs (6 variables × 6 hours) with no day-of-year nodes, inconsistent with the 38-dimensional input vector defined in Eq. 2/Eq. 5 and stated in Sec. 1/Sec. 2.1/Sec. 3.1. See Major issue 2.
- **Fig. 3/4 (reliability diagrams, `Relia_6h_MLP_MRMS_3panel_2025011000_to_2025123112_{12h,48h}.png`)**: all six BSS values quoted in the text (lines 462–465: 0.536/0.517, 0.460/0.450, 0.291/0.288 at 12 h; 0.423/0.382, 0.331/0.320, 0.177/0.173 at 48 h) match the legends printed on the actual figure panels exactly. The specific reliability percentages quoted in prose (90.8%/78.2% at 12 h 0.25 mm 90% bin; 94.7%/72.7% at 48 h) are visually consistent with the plotted curves. No discrepancy found here.
- **Fig. 5/6 (performance diagrams, `PerfDiagram_6h_MLP_MRMS_3panel_2025011000_to_2025123112_{12h,48h}.png`)**: visually confirm the Results-section text — MLP and control curves nearly overlap at both lead times/all thresholds, and at 10 mm/12 h (panel c) the red (control) curve does sit marginally up/right of the blue (MLP) curve over the mid-POD range, exactly as described (lines 524–526). This part of the text is accurate. The problem is that the *Summary* section (lines 641–646) later mischaracterizes this same result — see Major issue 1.
- **Fig. 6 (4-panel case study, `Figs_6h/6h_MLP_4panel_IC2025120412_lead36h.png`)**: the plot's own title banner ("31 to 36-h GRAF-based 6-h probabilistic forecasts, valid 00 UTC 06 Dec 2025") is arithmetically consistent with the caption's stated initial condition (12 UTC 4 Dec 2025) and 36-h lead time (12 UTC 4 Dec + 36 h = 00 UTC 6 Dec). The marked dot location is consistent across Figs. 6 and 7. Panel (a)'s broad GRAF precipitation region over the Pacific Northwest, and panels (b)–(d)'s terrain-following probability bands, are consistent with the caption/prose description (lines 566–573). One presentation note: panels (b)–(d) show a noticeably finer-grained, speckled probability texture than the smoother deterministic field in panel (a); this is not remarked on in the text and might prompt a question from a careful reader about whether it reflects real small-scale structure or a rendering/blending artifact.
- **Fig. 7 (`Figs_6h/6h_sample_IC2025120412_lead36h.png`)**: matches its caption (six thin hourly curves, one thick six-hourly MLP curve, correct IC/lead label "IC 2025120412, lead 36 h" printed in the panel). See the minor-issues note above regarding the large visual gap between hourly and six-hourly distributions, which is correctly captioned but could benefit from a sentence of interpretive help for the reader.
- The example `BSS_leadtime_q0.5_2025030100_to_2025123118.png` file mentioned in the task instructions is **not** referenced anywhere in `method_6h.tex` (confirmed via grep of all `\includegraphics` calls) — it appears to belong to a different plot/manuscript in this repository, not this one.
